Analyst Comment: The explosive threat in Iowa has drawn sharp concerns all around, regarding how to best proceed. On the one hand, the explosive compound itself is reportedly extremely shock sensitive and can't be moved without fear of detonation. In these cases it's normal to blast the explosives in place (under controlled conditions) rather than risk lives trying to move the barrels. On the other hand, blanketing America's heartland with lead dust is also suboptimal. The crisis itself began back in March, when a routine security audit revealed that 68x drums full of explosives were being stored in an exceptionally dangerous state. The drums had been buried in two magazines for long term storage, and at some point the water bath storage system had failed, and the explosives had degraded to the point that an emergency now exists. Roughly 9,500 pounds of explosives now await mitigation, and the Army has decided that the safest course of action is to blow the magazines in place.

The Army has known about this threat for approximately 9 months, and in that time little effort has been made to mitigate the threat, with locals only finding out about it a few days before detonation. Regardless, this morning, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle stated that a "water wall" has been constructed around the blast site, to potentially mitigate the spread of lead dust. This confirms beyond all doubt that the Army had mitigation tools at their disposal, but chose not to use them, only enacting mitigation efforts due to the public outcry.

Nevertheless, the clock is ticking and the Iowa HHS Department has given less than 24 hours notice for tens of thousands of people spread out over hundreds of miles throughout Iowa to evacuate or shelter in place before tomorrows blast. FEMA officials have gone door to door in the blast zone, handing out nothing more than rolls of duct tape and informational flyers. None of the plume analysis models regarding the lead dust cloud's potential spread have been released to the public, and local weathermen are just having to guess where the dust cloud might go based on the sensors that they have.

This situation is made more concerning by the fact that the Army is not actually disclosing the full contents of what is to be detonated. The emergency EPA report is heavily redacted, and some of the contents of the drums of explosives are blacked out, with "Lead Azide" being the only phrase that the public has to make decisions with. Heavy metal poisoning and the ecological disaster of farmland contamination aside, the public doesn't even fully know what the Army is planning to blast in place tomorrow morning, so the danger might be more than is being let on. Some of the redactions in the disclosure document make sense and are fairly routine, such as serial numbers and sensitive information related to the site itself. However, the entire point of redactions is to conceal information, and for those seeking to conduct their own risk analysis of the situation, it is unwise to risk heavy metal poisoning (or some other unknown ailment) based on a guess of what could be hidden under a redaction mark on a document. As such, until truth and transparency is established regarding this incident, locals will have to make decisions in an environment of uncertainty.
